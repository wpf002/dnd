# PART I — ORIGINS AND HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT

## 1. WARGAMING ROOTS

Dungeons & Dragons emerged from the miniature-wargaming hobby of the 1950s, 1960s, and early 1970s.

Traditional miniature wargames simulated battles between armies. Players controlled military units rather than individual heroes. Rules focused on:

• Movement
• Range
• Formation
• Morale
• Terrain
• Casualties
• Command decisions

Companies such as Avalon Hill helped establish commercial strategy gaming, while hobby clubs developed increasingly detailed miniature rules.

Gary Gygax became active in the American wargaming community and helped organize the first Gen Con in Lake Geneva, Wisconsin. His work on fantasy combat rules contributed directly to the development of D&D.

Dave Arneson experimented with something more personal. In his Blackmoor campaign, players controlled individual characters who:

• Explored dangerous locations
• Gained experience
• Improved over time
• Collected equipment
• Interacted with recurring characters
• Participated in an ongoing fictional world

This shift from commanding armies to inhabiting individual characters was the decisive conceptual breakthrough.

## 2. CHAINMAIL

Gary Gygax and Jeff Perren developed Chainmail, a medieval miniature wargaming system.

Chainmail included a fantasy supplement covering creatures and concepts such as:

• Heroes
• Wizards
• Dragons
• Elves
• Goblins
• Trolls
• Elementals

Although Chainmail was still a wargame, its fantasy material provided part of the mechanical foundation from which D&D developed.

## 3. BLACKMOOR

Dave Arneson's Blackmoor campaign introduced several practices that became central to tabletop role-playing:

• Persistent characters
• Character advancement
• Individual equipment
• Dungeon exploration
• Referee-controlled worlds
• Hidden information
• Improvised adjudication
• Continuing campaigns

Blackmoor was not merely a battle scenario. It was an evolving fictional environment in which player decisions accumulated consequences.

## 4. THE BIRTH OF DUNGEONS & DRAGONS

In 1974, Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson published the original Dungeons & Dragons boxed set through Tactical Studies Rules, commonly known as TSR.

The set contained three booklets:

• Men & Magic
• Monsters & Treasure
• The Underworld & Wilderness Adventures

The rules were incomplete by later standards and assumed familiarity with wargaming conventions. Dungeon Masters were expected to interpret, improvise, and expand the system.

The core innovation was clear:

Players controlled individual fantasy adventurers rather than armies.

These characters could:

• Explore dungeons
• Fight monsters
• Discover treasure
• Gain experience
• Increase in level
• Acquire magical items
• Develop reputations
• Influence a continuing world

## 5. THE DUNGEON MASTER

D&D established the role of the Dungeon Master, or DM.

The DM performs several functions:

• World creator
• Referee
• Scenario designer
• Adversary controller
• Rules adjudicator
• Narrator
• Keeper of hidden information
• Interpreter of player action

This role distinguished tabletop role-playing from conventional board games.

The DM does not merely administer predetermined rules. The DM responds to unexpected decisions and determines how the fictional world reacts.

## 6. WHY D&D WAS REVOLUTIONARY

D&D combined several ideas that had not previously been unified into one commercial game:

• Tactical conflict
• Improvised storytelling
• Persistent characters
• Experience-based advancement
• Open-ended exploration
• Referee adjudication
• Shared fictional worlds
• Cooperative problem-solving

It created the tabletop role-playing game as a recognizable form.

# PART II — EDITION HISTORY

## 1. ORIGINAL DUNGEONS & DRAGONS — 1974

The original game was compact, flexible, and loosely organized.

Core classes:

• Fighting-Man
• Magic-User
• Cleric

The Thief was added shortly afterward in the Greyhawk supplement.

Early play emphasized:

• Dungeon exploration
• Mapping
• Resource management
• Hirelings
• Treasure recovery
• Survival
• Player ingenuity

Characters were vulnerable. Combat was dangerous. Avoidance and negotiation were often more effective than direct confrontation.

## 2. EARLY SUPPLEMENTS — 1975 TO 1976

Major supplements included:

• Greyhawk
• Blackmoor
• Eldritch Wizardry
• Gods, Demi-Gods & Heroes

These books expanded:

• Classes
• Spells
• Monsters
• Psionics
• Artifacts
• Combat rules
• Campaign concepts

The game rapidly evolved through supplements, magazines, convention play, and local house rules.

## 3. BASIC D&D AND ADVANCED D&D

By 1977, D&D divided into two major lines.

**BASIC DUNGEONS & DRAGONS**

Designed as a more accessible introduction.

Major Basic editions and revisions included:

• Holmes Basic
• Moldvay Basic
• Cook/Marsh Expert
• Mentzer Basic, Expert, Companion, Master, and Immortal
• Rules Cyclopedia

Basic D&D often treated certain fantasy peoples as classes. An elf, dwarf, or halfling could be a complete class rather than a species combined with a profession.

**ADVANCED DUNGEONS & DRAGONS**

AD&D attempted to create a more standardized and comprehensive version.

The three foundational hardcovers were:

• Monster Manual
• Player's Handbook
• Dungeon Master's Guide

AD&D added greater complexity in:

• Combat
• Saving throws
• Alignment
• Class restrictions
• Spellcasting
• Equipment
• Initiative
• Character advancement

## 4. AD&D FIRST EDITION

AD&D 1st Edition established many conventions associated with classic D&D.

Notable features:

• Detailed class structures
• Race and class restrictions
• Alignment requirements
• Separate saving throw categories
• Descending Armor Class
• Complex initiative procedures
• Powerful high-level magic
• Extensive random tables
• Strong Dungeon Master authority

Major classes and subclasses included:

• Fighter
• Paladin
• Ranger
• Magic-User
• Illusionist
• Cleric
• Druid
• Thief
• Assassin
• Monk
• Bard

AD&D 1e supported a mixture of:

• Dungeon survival
• Wilderness exploration
• Domain play
• Political conflict
• High-level warfare
• Planar adventure

## 5. THE 1980s EXPANSION

During the 1980s, D&D expanded beyond rules into a large entertainment and publishing ecosystem.

Major developments included:

• Campaign settings
• Adventure modules
• Novels
• Dragon Magazine
• Dungeon Magazine
• Licensed toys
• Animation
• Computer games

Major settings included:

• Greyhawk
• Dragonlance
• Forgotten Realms
• Mystara
• Ravenloft

D&D also became the subject of moral panic. Critics associated the game with occultism, violence, and psychological danger. These claims lacked credible evidence, but the controversy significantly affected the game's public image.

TSR altered some terminology and presentation during this period. Demons and devils were later renamed tanar'ri and baatezu in 2nd Edition products.

## 6. AD&D SECOND EDITION — 1989

AD&D 2nd Edition reorganized and revised the game.

Major changes included:

• Cleaner presentation
• Consolidated class groups
• Removal or reworking of controversial material
• Expanded nonweapon proficiencies
• Specialist wizards
• Priest spheres
• Character kits
• Extensive setting support

Class groups included:

• Warrior
• Wizard
• Priest
• Rogue

Second Edition became known for setting depth and large numbers of supplements.

Major settings included:

• Forgotten Realms
• Dragonlance
• Ravenloft
• Dark Sun
• Spelljammer
• Planescape
• Birthright
• Al-Qadim

Its strengths were breadth, atmosphere, and worldbuilding.

Its weaknesses included:

• Inconsistent subsystems
• Rules spread across many books
• Balance problems
• Product-line fragmentation

## 7. TSR'S DECLINE

During the 1990s, TSR expanded aggressively.

Problems included:

• Too many competing product lines
• High printing costs
• Inventory problems
• Weak market forecasting
• Internal management failures
• Retail returns
• Fragmented audiences

Despite strong creative output, TSR entered severe financial difficulty.

## 8. WIZARDS OF THE COAST ACQUIRES TSR — 1997

Wizards of the Coast acquired TSR in 1997.

Wizards had become highly successful through Magic: The Gathering and had the resources to stabilize D&D.

The acquisition led to:

• Consolidation of product lines
• Revised business practices
• Development of a unified new edition
• Greater focus on organized rules architecture
• Integration of TSR properties into Wizards

## 9. DUNGEONS & DRAGONS THIRD EDITION — 2000

Third Edition replaced the Basic/Advanced divide with a unified D&D line.

Its defining innovation was the d20 System.

Core mechanic:

Roll a twenty-sided die, add modifiers, and compare the result to a target number.

This structure standardized:

• Attacks
• Saving throws
• Skills
• Ability checks
• Spell resistance
• Special actions

Major features:

• Ascending Armor Class
• Feats
• Skills
• Multiclassing
• Prestige classes
• Challenge Rating
• Unified monster statistics
• Attacks of opportunity
• Detailed character construction

Third Edition emphasized player customization and mechanical transparency.

## 10. THE OPEN GAME LICENSE

Wizards released portions of the d20 rules through the Open Game License.

This allowed third-party publishers to create compatible material.

The result was an enormous expansion of:

• Adventures
• Campaign settings
• Classes
• Monsters
• Rules supplements
• Independent games

The OGL became one of the most consequential publishing decisions in tabletop role-playing history.

## 11. DUNGEONS & DRAGONS 3.5 — 2003

Version 3.5 revised Third Edition.

It changed:

• Class abilities
• Spells
• Feats
• Monsters
• Combat procedures
• Skills
• Polymorph effects
• Damage reduction

3.5 became famous for deep character optimization.

Strengths:

• Extraordinary customization
• Extensive third-party support
• Detailed tactical rules
• Strong mechanical interoperability

Weaknesses:

• Rules complexity
• Stacking modifiers
• Preparation burden
• High-level caster dominance
• Optimization disparities

## 12. EBERRON — 2004

Wizards held a campaign-setting search that resulted in Eberron, created by Keith Baker.

Eberron combined:

• Pulp adventure
• Noir intrigue
• Industrialized magic
• Postwar politics
• Airships
• Lightning rail
• Warforged
• Dragonmarked houses

It demonstrated that D&D could support settings far beyond conventional medieval fantasy.

## 13. DUNGEONS & DRAGONS FOURTH EDITION — 2008

Fourth Edition redesigned D&D around tactical clarity and class balance.

Major features:

• At-will powers
• Encounter powers
• Daily powers
• Defined combat roles
• Healing surges
• Grid-based movement
• Standardized monster roles
• Skill challenges
• Tiered progression

Class roles included:

• Defender
• Striker
• Leader
• Controller

Fourth Edition made combat highly structured and team-oriented.

Strengths:

• Strong tactical balance
• Dynamic encounter design
• Clear monster construction
• Meaningful battlefield movement
• Capable noncaster classes

Criticisms:

• Repetitive power structure
• Heavy emphasis on combat
• Reduced mechanical distinction between some classes
• Presentation that resembled digital game logic
• Limited compatibility with earlier editions

## 14. PATHFINDER

Paizo Publishing created Pathfinder using the 3.5 rules framework under the Open Game License.

Pathfinder attracted players who preferred:

• 3.5-style customization
• Dense mechanical systems
• Traditional class design
• Adventure Path campaigns

For a period, Pathfinder became D&D's principal commercial rival.

## 15. DUNGEONS & DRAGONS FIFTH EDITION — 2014

Fifth Edition attempted to reunify the audience.

Its design emphasized:

• Accessibility
• Fast adjudication
• Familiar class identities
• Reduced modifier stacking
• Bounded accuracy
• Advantage and disadvantage
• Backgrounds
• Subclasses
• Flexible rulings

Core classes:

• Barbarian
• Bard
• Cleric
• Druid
• Fighter
• Monk
• Paladin
• Ranger
• Rogue
• Sorcerer
• Warlock
• Wizard

The Artificer was later added through Eberron material.

Fifth Edition supported both tactical play and narrative campaigns without requiring the detailed system mastery of 3.5 or the rigid combat structure of 4e.

## 16. THE FIFTH EDITION RENAISSANCE

Several forces expanded D&D's audience:

• Streaming
• Actual-play shows
• D&D Beyond
• Virtual tabletops
• Stranger Things
• Organized play
• Online communities
• Accessible starter products
• Celebrity participation

Prominent actual-play productions included:

• Critical Role
• Dimension 20
• The Adventure Zone
• Not Another D&D Podcast

D&D became a form of entertainment for viewers as well as players.

## 17. THE 2024 CORE RULE REVISION

Wizards revised the core Fifth Edition rules through new editions of the Player's Handbook, Dungeon Master's Guide, and Monster Manual.

The revision retained the Fifth Edition framework rather than replacing it with a completely separate edition.

Major areas of revision included:

• Class redesign
• Revised subclasses
• Weapon mastery
• Updated feats
• Updated backgrounds
• Species terminology
• Spell revisions
• Revised monster presentation
• Rules clarification
• Improved book organization

The revised game remained broadly connected to the 2014 system.

## 18. D&D AS A PLATFORM

Modern D&D is no longer only a printed ruleset.

It also functions through:

• Digital character management
• Online rule libraries
• Virtual tabletops
• Streaming media
• Video games
• Organized play
• Community publishing
• Licensed entertainment

The game now operates as a transmedia fantasy ecosystem.

# PART IV — WORLDS AND CAMPAIGN SETTINGS

## 1. GREYHAWK

**Creator:** Gary Gygax
**Primary Region:** The Flanaess on the world of Oerth
**Tone:** Sword-and-sorcery, political rivalry, dangerous wilderness, lost empires

Greyhawk grew from Gygax's home campaign.

Major elements:

• The Free City of Greyhawk
• The Circle of Eight
• Iuz
• The Great Kingdom
• The Temple of Elemental Evil
• The Tomb of Horrors
• Castle Greyhawk

Many famous spell names derive from characters associated with Greyhawk:

• Mordenkainen
• Bigby
• Tenser
• Otiluke
• Rary
• Nystul
• Drawmij

Greyhawk remains closely associated with early D&D's blend of dungeon exploration, political conflict, and morally ambiguous adventure.

## 2. BLACKMOOR

**Creator:** Dave Arneson
**Tone:** Experimental fantasy, ancient technology, dungeon exploration

Blackmoor predates published D&D as a campaign.

It is historically important because it introduced many practices that became foundational to role-playing.

Later versions incorporated:

• Lost technology
• Science-fantasy elements
• Ancient civilizations
• Political conflict
• Large dungeon complexes

## 3. MYSTARA

**Origins:** Basic D&D's Known World
**Tone:** Classic fantasy sandbox with diverse nations and cultures

Mystara developed through Basic D&D modules and Gazetteers.

Major regions and concepts:

• The Known World
• Karameikos
• Glantri
• Thyatis
• Alphatia
• Hollow World
• The Savage Coast

Mystara frequently emphasizes:

• Exploration
• National cultures
• Trade
• Immortals rather than conventional gods
• Accessible campaign structure

## 4. FORGOTTEN REALMS

**Creator:** Ed Greenwood
**Primary Continent:** Faerûn
**Tone:** High fantasy, deep history, active gods, powerful magic, political intrigue

Forgotten Realms became D&D's most widely used official setting.

Major locations:

• Waterdeep
• Baldur's Gate
• Neverwinter
• Icewind Dale
• Cormyr
• The Dalelands
• The Underdark
• Thay
• Chult

Major figures:

• Elminster
• Drizzt Do'Urden
• Laeral Silverhand
• Szass Tam
• Manshoon
• Mystra
• Volo
• Minsc

Major themes:

• Divine intervention
• Ancient magical empires
• Competing factions
• Regional diversity
• Forgotten ruins
• Political and religious conflict

Forgotten Realms serves as the setting for major games such as:

• Baldur's Gate
• Icewind Dale
• Neverwinter Nights
• Baldur's Gate 3

## 5. DRAGONLANCE

**Creators:** Tracy Hickman and Margaret Weis, with the original design team
**Primary Continent:** Ansalon on the world of Krynn
**Tone:** Epic war, heroic tragedy, faith, sacrifice, moral balance

Dragonlance was designed as a coordinated narrative project across adventures and novels.

Major elements:

• The War of the Lance
• The Cataclysm
• The return of the gods
• Dragon armies
• Dragonlances
• Knights of Solamnia
• Orders of High Sorcery

Major characters:

• Raistlin Majere
• Caramon Majere
• Tanis Half-Elven
• Sturm Brightblade
• Laurana
• Tasslehoff Burrfoot
• Kitiara
• Lord Soth

Dragonlance helped establish the model of D&D as epic serialized fantasy.

## 6. RAVENLOFT

**Origins:** The 1983 adventure I6 Ravenloft
**Tone:** Gothic horror, tragedy, corruption, imprisonment, doomed villains
**Primary concept:** The Domains of Dread

Each domain is ruled by a Darklord trapped within a supernatural prison shaped by that ruler's crimes and obsessions.

Major elements:

• Barovia
• Strahd von Zarovich
• The Mists
• Darklords
• Curses
• Moral horror
• Psychological corruption

Ravenloft transformed horror monsters from simple enemies into tragic, symbolic, and psychologically complex antagonists.

## 7. SPELLJAMMER

**Tone:** Space fantasy, exploration, piracy, cosmic adventure

Spelljammer connects fantasy worlds through magical travel.

Major elements:

• Spelljamming ships
• Wildspace
• Astral travel
• Giff
• Neogi
• Mind flayers
• Spacefaring elves
• Cosmic monsters

Classic Spelljammer used crystal spheres and the phlogiston. Later interpretations revised parts of this cosmology.

Spelljammer allows D&D campaigns to combine:

• Fantasy
• Naval adventure
• Planetary exploration
• Cosmic horror
• Swashbuckling

## 8. DARK SUN

**World:** Athas
**Tone:** Post-apocalyptic survival, scarcity, tyranny, environmental ruin

Athas is a dying world devastated by destructive magic.

Major elements:

• Defiling magic
• Preserving magic
• Sorcerer-kings
• City-states
• Psionics
• Scarce metal
• Slavery
• Harsh deserts
• Mutated creatures

Dark Sun radically changes familiar D&D assumptions:

• Gods are absent
• Resources are scarce
• Magic is feared
• Survival is difficult
• Civilization is oppressive
• Heroism is costly

## 9. PLANESCAPE

**Primary Hub:** Sigil, the City of Doors
**Tone:** Philosophical fantasy, surrealism, planar politics, belief-driven reality

Planescape treats the multiverse as a setting for ideological conflict.

Major elements:

• Sigil
• The Lady of Pain
• Portals
• Factions
• Outer Planes
• Fiendish politics
• Gods
• Petitioners
• Belief shaping reality

Planescape asks philosophical questions such as:

• What is truth?
• Can belief alter reality?
• Are gods worthy of worship?
• Does alignment determine morality?
• Can identity survive transformation?

## 10. BIRTHRIGHT

**World:** Cerilia
**Tone:** Dynastic politics, rulership, war, divine bloodlines

Players may rule:

• Kingdoms
• Temples
• Guilds
• Magical domains

Major concepts:

• Bloodlines
• Regency
• Domain turns
• Political alliances
• Mass warfare
• Dynastic legitimacy

Birthright extends D&D from personal adventure into governance and statecraft.

## 11. AL-QADIM

**Region:** Zakhara
**Tone:** Romance, fate, desert adventure, courts, trade, genies

Al-Qadim was connected to Forgotten Realms but functioned as a distinct cultural setting.

Major elements:

• Genies
• Desert kingdoms
• Corsairs
• Merchant cities
• Fate
• Honor
• Courtly intrigue

## 12. KARA-TUR

**Region:** Eastern lands of the Forgotten Realms
**Tone:** East Asian-inspired fantasy

Major elements:

• Imperial courts
• Monasteries
• Spirits
• Ancestor traditions
• Martial arts
• Regional political systems

## 13. MAZTICA

**Region:** Western lands of the Forgotten Realms
**Tone:** Mesoamerican-inspired fantasy and colonial conflict

Maztica explored:

• Empire
• Conquest
• Religion
• Cultural encounter
• Indigenous mythologies

Its original treatment has been criticized for reductive and colonial framing.

## 14. EBERRON

**Creator:** Keith Baker
**Primary Continent:** Khorvaire
**Tone:** Pulp adventure, noir intrigue, industrial magic, postwar tension

Major elements:

• The Last War
• Sharn
• Dragonmarked Houses
• Warforged
• Lightning rail
• Elemental airships
• The Mournland
• Espionage
• National rivalry

Eberron rejects simple alignment assumptions.

A traditionally good creature may be corrupt.
A traditionally evil creature may be honorable.
Religion is not conclusively verified.
Magic functions as infrastructure and industry.

## 15. POINTS OF LIGHT

**Edition:** Fourth Edition
**Tone:** Heroic civilization surrounded by dangerous wilderness

The default 4e world emphasized isolated settlements separated by ruins, monsters, and unstable frontiers.

Major elements:

• Nentir Vale
• Nerath
• Arkhosia
• Bael Turath
• Feywild
• Shadowfell
• Dawn War
• Primordials
• Core pantheon

## 16. THEROS

**Origin:** Magic: The Gathering
**Tone:** Greek-inspired mythic fantasy

Major elements:

• Active gods
• Heroic destiny
• Omens
• Mythic monsters
• Divine devotion
• Underworld journeys

Theros uses supernatural gifts and piety to connect characters directly to mythic structures.

## 17. RAVNICA

**Origin:** Magic: The Gathering
**Tone:** Urban fantasy, guild politics, magical metropolis

Ravnica is a world-spanning city organized around ten powerful guilds.

Campaigns emphasize:

• Bureaucracy
• Espionage
• Faction loyalty
• Urban adventure
• Magical infrastructure
• Ideological conflict

## 18. EXANDRIA

**Creator:** Matthew Mercer
**Origin:** Critical Role
**Tone:** Modern high fantasy, divine history, political conflict, character drama

Major regions:

• Tal'Dorei
• Wildemount
• Marquet
• Issylra

Exandria demonstrates the influence of streamed campaigns on modern D&D publishing and fandom.

# PART V — MULTIVERSAL COSMOLOGY

## 1. THE MATERIAL PLANE

The Material Plane contains ordinary physical worlds such as:

• Toril
• Oerth
• Krynn
• Eberron
• Athas

Different settings may use different cosmological models, but the Material Plane generally represents mortal reality.

## 2. THE ETHEREAL PLANE

The Ethereal Plane overlaps the Material Plane.

Common functions:

• Ghostly movement
• Transition to inner planes
• Hauntings
• Invisible observation
• Demiplanes
• Spiritual travel

## 3. THE ASTRAL PLANE

The Astral Plane is associated with:

• Thought
• Divine realms
• Interplanar travel
• Dead gods
• Psychic phenomena
• Astral vessels

Modern interpretations often use the Astral Sea as the medium connecting worlds and divine domains.

## 4. THE FEYWILD

The Feywild is an intensified reflection of the natural world.

Characteristics:

• Heightened emotion
• Ancient magic
• Strange time
• Archfey
• Beauty
• Danger
• Symbolic geography

The Feywild represents life, passion, enchantment, and untamed possibility.

## 5. THE SHADOWFELL

The Shadowfell is a dark reflection associated with:

• Death
• Memory
• Entropy
• Sorrow
• Undeath
• Despair

It contains domains and entities tied to mortality, loss, and spiritual decay.

## 6. THE INNER PLANES

The Inner Planes embody fundamental material forces.

Traditional elemental planes:

• Air
• Earth
• Fire
• Water

Additional models may include:

• Positive Energy
• Negative Energy
• Para-elemental planes
• Quasi-elemental planes
• Elemental Chaos

## 7. THE OUTER PLANES

The Outer Planes embody alignment, ideology, faith, and metaphysical order.

Common examples:

• Mount Celestia
• Bytopia
• Elysium
• The Beastlands
• Arborea
• Ysgard
• Limbo
• Pandemonium
• The Abyss
• Carceri
• Hades
• Gehenna
• The Nine Hells
• Acheron
• Mechanus
• Arcadia

These planes are not merely places. They are moral and philosophical conditions made spatial.

## 8. SIGIL

Sigil is the City of Doors.

Characteristics:

• Located at the center of the multiverse in Planescape tradition
• Filled with portals
• Ruled by the Lady of Pain
• Closed to direct divine control
• Home to competing factions
• Neutral ground for planar travelers

Sigil turns cosmology into urban adventure.

## 9. THE FAR REALM

The Far Realm lies beyond conventional reality.

It is associated with:

• Alien geometry
• Madness
• Aberrations
• Unknowable entities
• Distorted causality
• Cosmic horror

Creatures such as mind flayers and beholders are often connected, directly or indirectly, to Far Realm themes.

## 10. WORLD AXIS COSMOLOGY

Fourth Edition used a simplified structure:

**Above:** The Astral Sea and divine dominions

**Center:** The mortal world, Feywild, and Shadowfell

**Below:** The Elemental Chaos and Abyss

This cosmology emphasized mythic conflict between gods and primordials.

## 11. EBERRON COSMOLOGY

Eberron uses orbiting planes whose influence changes as they move closer to or farther from the world.

# PART IX — MAGIC AND SPELLCASTING SYSTEMS

## 1. VANCIAN MAGIC

Early D&D spellcasting was strongly influenced by Jack Vance's Dying Earth fiction.

Core concept:

A spellcaster prepares a specific spell, casts it, and then loses access to that prepared instance until preparing again.

This created:

• Scarcity
• Planning
• Risk
• Strong resource management
• High consequences for poor preparation

## 2. SPELL LEVELS

D&D organized spells into levels of increasing power.

Arcane spells traditionally ranged from levels 1 through 9.

Cantrips later became level 0 spells.

Spell levels are separate from character levels.

Examples:

1st level:
• Magic Missile
• Shield
• Sleep

3rd level:
• Fireball
• Fly
• Counterspell

5th level:
• Teleportation Circle
• Raise Dead
• Wall of Force

9th level:
• Wish
• Meteor Swarm
• Gate
• Time Stop

## 3. ARCANE AND DIVINE MAGIC

**ARCANE MAGIC**

Traditionally associated with:

• Wizards
• Sorcerers
• Warlocks
• Bards
• Artificers

Sources may include:

• Study
• Inborn power
• Pacts
• Performance
• Magical engineering

**DIVINE MAGIC**

Traditionally associated with:

• Clerics
• Paladins

Sources may include:

• Gods
• Oaths
• Sacred ideals
• Cosmic forces
• Faith

## 4. PRIMAL MAGIC

Primal magic is associated with:

• Nature
• Spirits
• Beasts
• Elements
• Seasons
• Life cycles

Primary users:

• Druids
• Rangers

## 5. PSIONICS

Psionics represents power generated through mind, consciousness, or psychic discipline.

Common abilities:

• Telepathy
• Telekinesis
• Precognition
• Psychic attack
• Metacreativity
• Body alteration

Psionics has used several different systems across editions:

• Percentile-based powers
• Separate attack and defense modes
• Power points
• Class abilities
• Psionic subclasses

## 6. SCHOOLS OF MAGIC

**ABJURATION** — Protection, wards, dispelling

**CONJURATION** — Summoning, teleportation, creation

**DIVINATION** — Knowledge, prediction, detection

**ENCHANTMENT** — Mental influence, charm, compulsion

**EVOCATION** — Energy, force, elemental destruction

**ILLUSION** — Deception, sensory alteration, shadow

**NECROMANCY** — Death, life force, undeath

**TRANSMUTATION** — Transformation of matter and creatures

## 7. SORCERERS AND SPONTANEOUS CASTING

Third Edition introduced the Sorcerer as a spontaneous caster.

Unlike Wizards, Sorcerers knew fewer spells but could cast them flexibly without preparing each exact use.

This established a major distinction:

Wizard: Broad knowledge, preparation, versatility

Sorcerer: Limited known spells, flexible casting, innate power

## 8. WARLOCK PACT MAGIC

Warlocks gain magic through a relationship with a patron.

Patron types may include:

• Fiends
• Archfey
• Great Old Ones
• Celestials
• Genies
• Undead powers
• Hexblade forces

Warlock design emphasizes:

• Short-rest spell recovery
• Eldritch Blast
• Invocations
• Pact boons
• Patron obligations

## 9. FOURTH EDITION POWER STRUCTURE

Fourth Edition organized abilities by frequency.

**AT-WILL** — Usable repeatedly

**ENCOUNTER** — Usable once per encounter or after a short rest

**DAILY** — Usable once per day

**UTILITY** — Movement, defense, support, or non-damage abilities

This system extended beyond magic and gave martial characters comparable ability structures.

## 10. FIFTH EDITION SPELLCASTING

Fifth Edition uses a flexible preparation model.

Prepared casters generally prepare a list of spells and use spell slots to cast any prepared spell of an appropriate level.

Major features:

• Cantrips
• Upcasting
• Concentration
• Ritual casting
• Spell slots
• Short-rest and long-rest recovery
• Saving throw DCs
• Spell attack rolls

## 11. CONCENTRATION

Concentration limits a caster to maintaining one major ongoing effect at a time.

This prevents excessive stacking of:

• Buffs
• Debuffs
• Summons
• Battlefield-control spells

Concentration became one of Fifth Edition's primary balancing mechanisms.

## 12. THE WEAVE

In Forgotten Realms, the Weave is the structure through which most mortal spellcasters access magic.

Mystra governs or embodies the Weave.

Related concepts:

• Dead magic zones
• Wild magic zones
• Shadow Weave
• Spellplague
• Mystra's deaths and returns

The Weave is setting-specific rather than a universal explanation for all D&D magic.

## 13. WILD MAGIC

Wild magic introduces unpredictable magical effects.

It may result from:

• Unstable spellcasting
• Damaged magical networks
• Planar influence
• Innate chaotic power
• Magical catastrophe

Wild surges can be:

• Beneficial
• Harmful
• Absurd
• Transformative
• Environmentally disruptive

## 14. MAGIC AS A WORLD SYSTEM

A setting's magic should answer:

• Who can use it?
• How is it learned?
• What does it cost?
• Who regulates it?
• Can it be inherited?
• Can it be industrialized?
• Can it be suppressed?
• Does it alter economics?
• Does it change warfare?
• Does it undermine religion?
• Can magic solve hunger, disease, or transportation?
• Why has magic not eliminated ordinary labor?

# PART XI — DICE, MATHEMATICS, AND CORE MECHANICS

## 1. POLYHEDRAL DICE

D&D uses:

• d4
• d6
• d8
• d10
• d12
• d20
• d100 or percentile dice

Notation:

2d6 means roll two six-sided dice.
1d20 means roll one twenty-sided die.
4d8 + 4 means roll four eight-sided dice and add four.

## 2. THE D20

The d20 became D&D's central resolution die.

Modern core formula:

d20 + modifier versus Difficulty Class or Armor Class

## 3. ABILITY SCORES

The six traditional ability scores are:

**STRENGTH** — Physical power

**DEXTERITY** — Agility, reflexes, coordination

**CONSTITUTION** — Endurance and physical resilience

**INTELLIGENCE** — Reasoning, memory, learned knowledge

**WISDOM** — Awareness, judgment, intuition

**CHARISMA** — Presence, force of personality, social influence

## 4. ABILITY MODIFIERS

Modern editions commonly derive a modifier from the ability score.

Typical values:

| Score | Modifier |
|---|---|
| 8–9 | −1 |
| 10–11 | +0 |
| 12–13 | +1 |
| 14–15 | +2 |
| 16–17 | +3 |
| 18–19 | +4 |
| 20 | +5 |

## 5. ARMOR CLASS

Early editions used descending Armor Class.

Lower AC was better.

Third Edition changed to ascending Armor Class.

Higher AC is better.

Attack resolution:

d20 + attack bonus versus Armor Class

## 6. THAC0

THAC0 means: To Hit Armor Class 0

It was used primarily in AD&D 2nd Edition.

A character's THAC0 and the target's descending Armor Class determined the required attack roll.

## 7. SAVING THROWS

Early editions used categories such as:

• Death
• Wands
• Paralysis
• Breath Weapon
• Spells

Third Edition used:

• Fortitude
• Reflex
• Will

Fifth Edition uses saving throws tied directly to all six abilities.

## 8. SKILLS

Skills formalized noncombat expertise.

Examples:

• Athletics
• Acrobatics
• Arcana
• History
• Investigation
• Nature
• Religion
• Perception
• Stealth
• Persuasion
• Deception
• Insight
• Survival

## 9. ADVANTAGE AND DISADVANTAGE

Fifth Edition replaced many small situational modifiers with:

**ADVANTAGE** — Roll two d20s and use the higher result.

**DISADVANTAGE** — Roll two d20s and use the lower result.

This system simplifies conditional bonuses and penalties.

## 10. BOUNDED ACCURACY

Fifth Edition limits numerical escalation.

Effects:

• Low-level creatures can remain relevant
• Armor Class remains within a narrower range
• Large groups can threaten stronger characters
• Bonuses are easier to track
• Difficulty remains more stable across levels

## 11. ACTION ECONOMY

Action economy describes how many meaningful actions each side can perform.

In Fifth Edition, a turn may include:

• Movement
• Action
• Bonus action
• Reaction
• Free interaction

A single boss is often disadvantaged against a full party because the party takes more total actions.

Legendary actions and lair actions help compensate.

## 12. EXPERIENCE AND LEVELS

Characters gain levels through:

• Experience points
• Milestone advancement
• Story-based advancement

Levels represent increasing:

• Durability
• Skill
• Magical power
• Influence
• Class abilities

## 13. HIT POINTS

Hit points abstract multiple concepts:

• Physical resilience
• Luck
• Stamina
• Defensive skill
• Will to continue
• Narrative protection

They are not necessarily literal meat or blood volume.

## 14. ALIGNMENT

Classic alignment uses two axes.

LAW versus CHAOS

GOOD versus EVIL

Nine alignments:

• Lawful Good
• Neutral Good
• Chaotic Good
• Lawful Neutral
• True Neutral
• Chaotic Neutral
• Lawful Evil
• Neutral Evil
• Chaotic Evil

Alignment has shifted from a restrictive mechanical system toward a descriptive role-playing tool.

# APPENDIX A — EDITION COMPARISON MATRIX

**ORIGINAL D&D**

Primary identity: Experimental dungeon adventure
Strengths: Freedom, danger, improvisation
Weaknesses: Ambiguous organization, heavy referee interpretation

**AD&D FIRST EDITION**

Primary identity: Comprehensive fantasy simulation
Strengths: Depth, atmosphere, strong campaign structure
Weaknesses: Complexity, inconsistency, high entry barrier

**BASIC D&D**

Primary identity: Accessible adventure and exploration
Strengths: Clarity, speed, campaign progression
Weaknesses: Limited initial options, race-as-class constraints

**AD&D SECOND EDITION**

Primary identity: Worldbuilding and campaign settings
Strengths: Lore, settings, kits, atmosphere
Weaknesses: Fragmented rules, supplement overload

**D&D THIRD EDITION**

Primary identity: Unified mechanical architecture
Strengths: Customization, consistency, interoperability
Weaknesses: Complexity and balance problems

**D&D 3.5**

Primary identity: System mastery and optimization
Strengths: Enormous depth, tactical precision
Weaknesses: Rules burden, high-level imbalance

**D&D FOURTH EDITION**

Primary identity: Tactical team combat
Strengths: Balance, encounter design, class parity
Weaknesses: Rigid structure, limited compatibility, combat emphasis

**D&D FIFTH EDITION**

Primary identity: Accessible collaborative fantasy
Strengths: Flexibility, simplicity, broad appeal
Weaknesses: Ambiguous rulings, inconsistent encounter math, limited high-level support

**2024 REVISED FIFTH EDITION**

Primary identity: Refined and reorganized Fifth Edition
Strengths: Updated classes, clearer presentation, improved compatibility
Weaknesses: Transition complexity, mixed legacy content, continued Fifth Edition structural limitations

# APPENDIX B — FOUNDATIONAL TERMS

**ADVENTURE** — A defined scenario or sequence of scenarios.

**CAMPAIGN** — A continuing series of adventures using persistent characters and world events.

**CHARACTER CLASS** — A mechanical and narrative profession or archetype.

**DUNGEON** — Any structured adventure environment composed of connected areas, challenges, and hidden information.

**DUNGEON MASTER** — The person who controls the world, adjudicates rules, and portrays nonplayer characters.

**ENCOUNTER** — A discrete challenge involving combat, exploration, negotiation, or hazard.

**HIT DICE** — Dice associated with health, level, or monster durability.

**LEVEL** — A measure of character or spell progression.

**MODULE** — A published adventure.

**NONPLAYER CHARACTER** — A character controlled by the Dungeon Master.

**PLAYER CHARACTER** — A character controlled by a player.

**ROLE-PLAYING** — Making decisions from the perspective of a fictional character.

**SETTING** — The fictional world or environment in which play occurs.

**SOURCEBOOK** — A publication containing rules, lore, options, or setting information.

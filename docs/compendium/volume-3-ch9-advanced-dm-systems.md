# I. ADVANCED DM PHILOSOPHY

As campaigns grow, the world should continue moving independently of the player characters.

The goal is not simulation for its own sake.

The goal is to create a world that feels alive.

Large-scale systems should answer:

• What changes between adventures?
• What happens if the heroes do nothing?
• Which factions gain or lose power?
• How does time affect the setting?

Use abstraction whenever possible.

The DM should spend time making decisions, not performing bookkeeping.

# II. TIME MANAGEMENT

Track time at multiple scales.

**TACTICAL**

• Seconds
• Combat rounds

**SCENE**

• Minutes
• Individual encounters

**SESSION**

• Hours
• One adventuring day

**CAMPAIGN**

• Days
• Weeks
• Months

**WORLD**

• Seasons
• Years
• Generations

Every important event should exist somewhere on this timeline.

# III. DOWNTIME SYSTEMS

Downtime provides opportunities that adventures cannot.

Characters may:

• Train
• Research
• Craft
• Build relationships
• Operate businesses
• Recruit followers
• Gather intelligence
• Heal
• Establish strongholds
• Advance faction goals

Downtime should create new stories rather than simply award bonuses.

---

# IV. TRAVEL SYSTEMS

Travel should occasionally matter.

Ask:

Why is this journey interesting?

Interesting travel may include:

• Navigation
• Weather
• Discovery
• Resource pressure
• Diplomacy
• Ambush
• Environmental hazards

Skip uneventful travel.

Play meaningful travel.

---

# V. HEXCRAWL DESIGN

A hex represents an area of exploration.

Each hex should contain one or more:

• Landmark
• Hazard
• Resource
• Rumor
• Settlement
• Monster territory
• Secret
• Dungeon
• Environmental feature

Players should gradually transform blank maps into known territory.

Discovery is the reward.

---

# VI. FACTION TURNS

Between adventures, each major faction takes one strategic action.

Possible actions:

• Expand
• Recruit
• Spy
• Attack
• Trade
• Negotiate
• Research
• Fortify
• Sabotage
• Recover

Resolve logically based on faction goals and available resources.

Track only meaningful changes.

# VII. KINGDOM MANAGEMENT

Track broad metrics instead of individual citizens.

Suggested ratings (1–10):

Population

Stability

Military

Treasury

Infrastructure

Trade

Agriculture

Arcane Influence

Religious Influence

Public Trust

Events change these values over time.

Examples:

Successful harvest: + Agriculture

Civil unrest: − Stability

Trade agreement: + Trade

Dragon attack: − Infrastructure, − Public Trust

# VIII. WARFARE & MASS COMBAT

Mass combat should emphasize decisions over dice volume.

Key factors:

• Leadership
• Morale
• Terrain
• Logistics
• Intelligence
• Supply
• Fortifications

Player characters influence battles by completing decisive objectives.

Examples:

• Eliminate enemy commander
• Destroy siege engines
• Secure bridge
• Break supply line
• Protect standard bearer
• Seal magical portal

The army's fate should not rely solely on one battle roll.

---

# IX. REPUTATION & RENOWN

Track reputation separately with each faction.

Suggested scale:

```
-5  Hated
-4  Hostile
-3  Distrusted
-2  Wary
-1  Unknown
 0  Neutral
+1  Recognized
+2  Trusted
+3  Honored
+4  Celebrated
+5  Legendary
```

Benefits should emerge naturally from reputation.

Examples:

• Discounts
• Political access
• Military support
• Religious favor
• Information
• Titles
• Land grants

# X. PROCEDURAL GENERATION

Prepare reusable generators instead of fixed content.

Generators may create:

• NPCs
• Taverns
• Shops
• Weather
• Rumors
• Encounters
• Dungeons
• Villages
• Noble houses
• Cults

This increases flexibility while reducing preparation time.

# THE DAVIS ADVANCED PRINCIPLE

The larger the campaign becomes, the less the Dungeon Master should attempt to control every detail.

Instead, design systems that naturally produce interesting outcomes.

A living world is not one where everything is tracked.

A living world is one where everything important can change.

# PART I — DUNGEON DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

## 1. WHAT A DUNGEON IS

A dungeon is any bounded adventure environment composed of connected spaces, hidden information, obstacles, inhabitants, and meaningful choices.

A dungeon does not need to be underground.

Possible dungeons include:

• Crypts
• Fortresses
• Temples
• Caves
• Ruined cities
• Haunted manors
• Wizard towers
• Forest labyrinths
• Ships
• Space stations
• Living creatures
• Dream realms
• Political palaces
• Planar prisons
• Flooded mines
• Abandoned laboratories

The defining characteristic is structured exploration.

## 2. THE THREE DESIGN MANDATES

Every dungeon should satisfy three forms of logic.

**FUNCTIONAL LOGIC**

Why was the location built or formed?

Examples:

• Temple
• Prison
• Mine
• Fortress
• Tomb
• Laboratory
• Refuge
• Palace
• Sewer
• Natural cave

**WORLD LOGIC**

Why does the location exist here?

Examples:

• The mine follows a mineral seam
• The fortress protects a river crossing
• The temple was built over a sacred spring
• The prison lies beneath the capital
• The cave system formed beneath volcanic rock

**STORY LOGIC**

Why are the characters entering now?

Examples:

• A prisoner must be rescued
• A sealed evil is awakening
• A relic has been discovered
• A rival faction arrived first
• The structure is collapsing
• A political deadline is approaching

## 3. THE DUNGEON AS A STORYTELLING DEVICE

A dungeon tells a story through space.

Its layout can reveal:

• Who built it
• What they valued
• What went wrong
• Who controls it now
• What has changed over time
• What danger remains
• What truth lies at its center

The strongest dungeons do not explain everything through journals and exposition.

They allow players to infer history from:

• Architecture
• Damage
• Repurposed rooms
• Bodies
• Symbols
• Traps
• Barricades
• Creature behavior
• Abandoned tools
• Changes in material

## 4. THE DUNGEON AS A DECISION ENGINE

A dungeon should produce repeated decisions.

Examples:

• Left passage or right passage
• Continue or retreat
• Spend resources or conserve them
• Fight or negotiate
• Open the sealed door or leave it alone
• Take treasure or avoid the curse
• Rescue captives or pursue the villain
• Rest in an unsafe area or press forward

A dungeon without decisions is a corridor with illustrations.

## 5. CHALLENGE THE PLAYERS AND CHARACTERS

Good dungeon design tests both:

**PLAYER CAPABILITIES**

• Reasoning
• Attention
• Planning
• Risk assessment
• Communication
• Creativity

**CHARACTER CAPABILITIES**

• Skills
• Spells
• Features
• Equipment
• Durability
• Background knowledge

Do not rely exclusively on either side.

A puzzle that ignores character abilities can feel disconnected from the game.

A challenge solved only by rolling one check can feel mechanically empty.

## 6. FAIRNESS

A dungeon may be dangerous without being arbitrary.

Fair danger includes:

• Warning signs
• Discoverable information
• Multiple responses
• Consistent rules
• Opportunities to retreat
• Consequences proportional to choices

Unfair danger includes:

• Undetectable instant death
• Contradictory clues
• Unavoidable punishment
• Retroactive rules
• Solutions requiring hidden DM assumptions

# PART II — DUNGEON PURPOSE AND ORIGIN

## 1. ORIGINAL PURPOSE

Before designing rooms, define the location's original purpose.

Questions:

• Who built it?
• Why was it built?
• Who funded it?
• Who used it?
• What activities occurred there?
• How was it supplied?
• How was it defended?
• Why was it abandoned or transformed?

## 2. CURRENT PURPOSE

The current inhabitants may use the dungeon differently.

Example:

Original purpose: Royal tomb

Current purpose: Smuggler headquarters

Design consequence:

• Burial chambers become storage
• Secret passages become escape routes
• Ritual halls become meeting chambers
• Undead remain in sealed sections
• Smugglers avoid cursed areas

## 3. HISTORICAL PHASES

A dungeon becomes richer when it has multiple historical layers.

Recommended structure:

**PHASE ONE — CONSTRUCTION**

Who created it and why?

**PHASE TWO — PRIMARY USE**

What did daily life look like?

**PHASE THREE — CRISIS**

What changed?

**PHASE FOUR — ABANDONMENT OR CONQUEST**

Why did control end?

**PHASE FIVE — CURRENT OCCUPATION**

Who lives there now?

**PHASE SIX — ACTIVE CHANGE**

What is happening during the adventure?

## 4. DUNGEON ORIGIN TYPES

**CONSTRUCTED**

Built intentionally.

Examples:

• Temple
• Fortress
• Palace
• Laboratory

**NATURAL**

Formed without deliberate construction.

Examples:

• Cave
• Volcano
• Glacier
• Coral reef

**TRANSFORMED**

A natural or ordinary place altered by magic or catastrophe.

Examples:

• Forest twisted by a curse
• City folded into another plane
• Mine infected by living crystal

**LIVING**

The dungeon is alive.

Examples:

• Colossal beast
• Sentient castle
• Fungal colony
• Organic spaceship

**SYMBOLIC**

The dungeon follows dream, memory, or metaphysical logic.

Examples:

• A dead king's regret
• A shared nightmare
• A trial created by a god
• A maze formed from forgotten memories

**FORTRESS**

Expected spaces:

• Gatehouse
• Walls
• Barracks
• Armory
• Command room
• Food storage
• Water source
• Stable
• Prison
• Escape route

**TEMPLE**

Expected spaces:

• Public sanctuary
• Ritual chamber
• Clergy quarters
• Reliquary
• Archive
• Pilgrim lodging
• Crypt
• Restricted sanctum

**MINE**

Expected spaces:

• Main shafts
• Side tunnels
• Support structures
• Equipment storage
• Ore processing
• Worker quarters
• Ventilation
• Drainage
• Emergency exits

**PALACE**

Expected spaces:

• Audience hall
• Private apartments
• Kitchens
• Servant corridors
• Gardens
• Treasury
• Guard posts
• Administrative offices
• Secret rooms

## 2. DEFENSIVE ARCHITECTURE

A defended location should use:

• Choke points
• Elevation
• Arrow slits
• Murder holes
• Barricades
• Controlled access
• Reinforcement routes
• Alarm systems
• Layered gates
• Kill zones

Defenders should understand their own environment.

## 3. DAILY-LIFE ARCHITECTURE

Ask how inhabitants:

• Eat
• Sleep
• Work
• Dispose of waste
• Store supplies
• Move goods
• Access water
• Communicate
• Heal injuries
• Respond to fire

A dungeon need not simulate every detail, but basic function prevents it from feeling artificial.

## 4. SACRED ARCHITECTURE

Sacred spaces may reflect theology.

Examples:

**SUN CULT**

• East-facing halls
• Open ceilings
• Gold surfaces
• Rising staircases

**DEATH CULT**

• Descending paths
• Repeated thresholds
• Bone-lined corridors
• Chambers aligned with funerary rites

**KNOWLEDGE CULT**

• Circular libraries
• Hidden indexes
• Symbolic geometry
• Restricted archives

**CHAOS CULT**

• Non-Euclidean rooms
• Contradictory paths
• Changing architecture
• Asymmetry

# PART IV — THE FIVE-LAYER DUNGEON MODEL

## 1. THRESHOLD

Purpose:

• Establish tone
• Present first danger
• Show the dungeon's rules
• Separate ordinary world from adventure space

Possible features:

• Gate
• Ruined entrance
• Guardian
• Warning
• Toll
• Puzzle
• Environmental transition

Questions:

• Why has the dungeon not already been looted?
• What stops ordinary people from entering?
• What first impression should players receive?

## 2. OUTER HALLS

Purpose:

• Introduce inhabitants
• Reveal initial history
• Provide low- to moderate-risk encounters
• Establish navigation options

Possible features:

• Guard rooms
• Storage
• Barracks
• Collapsed areas
• Recent camps
• Minor traps
• Patrol routes

## 3. INNER COMPLEX

Purpose:

• Deepen conflict
• Reveal faction structure
• Increase environmental complexity
• Introduce more dangerous opposition

Possible features:

• Workshops
• Ritual chambers
• Command centers
• Laboratories
• Major junctions
• Locked archives

## 4. DEPTHS

Purpose:

• Reveal corruption
• Escalate danger
• Break the assumptions established above
• Present the oldest or least controlled areas

Possible features:

• Ancient foundations
• Forbidden chambers
• Natural caverns
• Mutated ecosystems
• Planar breaches
• Sealed entities

## 5. CORE OR SANCTUM

Purpose:

• Deliver the central truth
• Present final opposition
• Force a decisive choice
• Resolve the dungeon's theme

Possible forms:

• Throne room
• Burial chamber
• Reactor
• Sacred heart
• Prison cell
• Ritual nexus
• Dragon lair
• Portal chamber

The core should not merely contain the strongest enemy.

It should contain the dungeon's meaning.

# PART V — DUNGEON TOPOLOGY AND NAVIGATION

## 1. LINEAR DESIGN

Structure:

Entrance → sequence of rooms → final chamber

Advantages:

• Easy to run
• Strong pacing
• Clear progression

Weaknesses:

• Limited agency
• Little replay value
• Retreat and flanking become less meaningful

Best used for:

• Short adventures
• Chases
• Ritual sequences
• Escape scenarios

## 2. BRANCHING DESIGN

Structure:

A main route with multiple side paths

Advantages:

• Moderate freedom
• Easier preparation
• Optional rewards
• Strong pacing control

Best used for most conventional dungeons.

## 3. LOOPING DESIGN

Structure:

Paths reconnect.

Advantages:

• Supports flanking
• Creates shortcuts
• Rewards mapping
• Allows alternative routes
• Makes retreat more interesting

Loops are one of the most valuable features in dungeon design.

## 4. HUB-AND-SPOKE DESIGN

Structure:

A central area connects to multiple zones.

Advantages:

• Clear orientation
• Flexible sequence
• Strong faction interaction
• Easy return to central safe or dangerous space

Examples:

• Central temple
• Underground lake
• Courtyard
• Transit nexus

## 5. OPEN NODE DESIGN

Structure:

Multiple interconnected locations without a single required path.

Advantages:

• High agency
• Strong replayability
• Strategic navigation

Weaknesses:

• Greater preparation burden
• Harder pacing
• More complex tracking

## 6. VERTICAL DESIGN

Use elevation as meaningful structure.

Examples:

• Towers
• Pits
• Cliff cities
• Hanging bridges
• Layered caverns
• Submerged levels

Verticality creates:

• Falling hazards
• Line-of-sight variation
• Alternative routes
• Environmental storytelling
• Tactical advantage

## 7. SECRET PATHS

Secret routes should have a reason to exist.

Possible purposes:

• Servant access
• Smuggling
• Emergency escape
• Ritual procession
• Maintenance
• Espionage
• Defense

Secret doors should not exist solely because dungeons traditionally contain secret doors.

## 8. MEANINGFUL CHOICE AT JUNCTIONS

A junction is useful when players have information.

Weak junction:

Two identical corridors.

Strong junction:

Left: Warm air, rhythmic hammering, smell of smoke

Right: Cold air, dripping water, distant whispering

The players do not know everything, but they have enough information to choose.

# PART VI — ZONES, ROOMS, AND FUNCTIONAL SPACE

## 1. ZONE DESIGN

Divide the dungeon into thematic zones.

Each zone should have:

• Function
• Visual identity
• Dominant inhabitants
• Environmental feature
• Tactical pattern
• Secret
• Transition to another zone

## 2. ROOM CATEGORIES

**TRANSIT ROOM**

Purpose: Movement between important locations

Examples:

• Hall
• Stairwell
• Bridge
• Shaft

**FUNCTIONAL ROOM**

Purpose: Supports daily use

Examples:

• Kitchen
• Barracks
• Workshop
• Storage

**CONFLICT ROOM**

Purpose: Contains active opposition

**DISCOVERY ROOM**

Purpose: Reveals lore, treasure, or hidden information

**DECISION ROOM**

Purpose: Presents choice, sacrifice, or branching consequence

**CLIMAX ROOM**

Purpose: Resolves a major sequence

## 3. THE ROOM VALUE TEST

Every room should provide at least one of the following:

• Decision
• Danger
• Discovery
• Resource
• Character interaction
• Tactical opportunity
• Atmospheric reinforcement

Rooms that provide none of these should be removed or combined.

## 4. ROOM DENSITY

Not every room needs combat.

A healthy mix may include:

• Combat rooms
• Empty but informative rooms
• Social rooms
• Hazard rooms
• Puzzle rooms
• Treasure rooms
• Transitional rooms

"Empty" rooms are useful when they:

• Build tension
• Reveal history
• Offer rest
• Provide clues
• Support navigation

## 5. LANDMARKS

Large dungeons need memorable landmarks.

Examples:

• Broken statue
• Underground waterfall
• Massive chain
• Glowing tree
• Bottomless shaft
• Suspended temple
• Fossilized dragon

Landmarks help players orient themselves.

# PART VII — DUNGEON ECOLOGY

## 1. ECOLOGICAL FOUNDATION

Ask:

• What provides energy or food?
• What consumes it?
• What scavenges remains?
• What controls population?
• What migrates?
• What is invasive?
• What is magical rather than natural?

## 2. THE ECOLOGICAL PYRAMID

**FOUNDATION**

Examples:

• Fungus
• Insects
• Algae
• Magical radiation
• Waste
• Imported supplies

**PRIMARY CONSUMERS**

Examples:

• Giant rats
• Beetles
• Herbivorous cave creatures

**PREDATORS**

Examples:

• Spiders
• Oozes
• Hunting beasts

**APEX CREATURES**

Examples:

• Dragon
• Aboleth
• Demon
• Beholder
• Powerful undead

## 3. HUMANOID SUPPLY

Intelligent inhabitants need:

• Food
• Water
• Fuel
• Weapons
• Medical supplies
• Waste disposal
• Replacement equipment

Possible explanations:

• Surface raids
• Trade
• Farming
• Magical creation
• Slavery
• Fungus cultivation
• Underground rivers

## 4. SYMBIOSIS

Examples:

• Oozes clean refuse
• Goblins cultivate fungi
• Spiders protect egg chambers
• Undead guard living cultists
• Bats spread magical spores
• Constructs maintain ancient machinery

## 5. TERRITORIAL LOGIC

Creatures should occupy areas suited to them.

A fire elemental in a flooded crypt requires explanation.

Possible explanations:

• Magical containment
• Recent summoning
• Volcanic fissure
• Elemental conflict
• Artificial furnace

## 6. ECOLOGY AS GAMEPLAY

Ecology can create solutions.

Examples:

• Lure predators toward enemies
• Poison a food source
• Follow scavengers to hidden chambers
• Use territorial markings to avoid danger
• Disrupt supply routes
• Befriend a neglected creature

**STRUCTURAL**

• Collapsing floors
• Weak bridges
• Rotating rooms
• Falling masonry
• Broken elevators

**MAGICAL**

• Wild magic surge
• Temporal distortion
• Teleportation loop
• Memory loss

**PSYCHOLOGICAL**

• Whispers
• Hallucinations
• Fear zones
• Dream intrusion
• Identity distortion

## 2. HAZARD DESIGN PRINCIPLES

A strong hazard has:

• Warning
• Trigger
• Effect
• Escalation
• Counterplay
• Consequence

## 3. HAZARDS AS TERRAIN

Hazards should interact with movement and combat.

Examples:

• Rising water
• Spreading fire
• Collapsing platforms
• Rotating blades
• Unstable magical zones
• Gravity shifts

## 4. ESCALATING HAZARDS

A hazard becomes more interesting when it changes over time.

Round 1: Smoke reduces visibility.

Round 2: Fire spreads.

Round 3: Support beams weaken.

Round 4: Exit becomes threatened.

## 5. NON-DAMAGE CONSEQUENCES

Hazards can cause:

• Separation
• Noise
• Lost equipment
• Exhaustion
• Delay
• Exposure
• Position changes
• Resource loss
• Attraction of enemies

# PART IX — TRAP DESIGN

## 1. PURPOSE OF TRAPS

Traps may:

• Defend
• Delay
• Alarm
• Capture
• Punish
• Test
• Preserve
• Contain
• Intimidate

The trap's design should reflect its purpose.

## 2. THE SIX-PART TRAP MODEL

**BUILDER** — Who created it?

**PURPOSE** — What is it intended to accomplish?

**SIGN** — What evidence reveals it?

**TRIGGER** — What activates it?

**EFFECT** — What happens?

**COUNTERPLAY** — How can it be avoided, disabled, redirected, or exploited?

## 3. FORESHADOWING

Fair traps usually provide clues.

Examples:

• Scratches on floor
• Burn marks
• Skeletons
• Holes in walls
• Strange odor
• Discolored stone
• Broken mechanism
• Repeated warning symbol

## 4. TRAP TYPES

**MECHANICAL**

• Blades
• Pits
• Darts
• Crushing walls
• Falling blocks

**ARCANE**

• Glyphs
• Curses
• Summoning circles
• Teleportation
• Magical fire

**ENVIRONMENTAL**

• Flooding
• Gas
• Cave-in
• Lava

**PSYCHOLOGICAL**

• Illusions
• False exits
• Memory alteration
• Paranoia

**MORAL**

• Sacrifice
• Betrayal
• Unequal rescue
• Temptation

## 5. TRAP SEVERITY

**SETBACK** — Consumes time, position, or minor resources.

**DANGEROUS** — Causes serious harm or tactical disadvantage.

**DEADLY** — Can incapacitate or kill.

Deadly traps require strong warning and meaningful counterplay.

## 6. ACTIVE TRAPS

A trap can function as an encounter rather than a single roll.

Examples:

• Room filling with sand
• Rotating hallway
• Descending ceiling
• Animated statue puzzle
• Moving blades
• Collapsing bridge

Players act across multiple rounds.

## 7. TRAP FAILURE

Failure should not always mean damage.

Possible outcomes:

• Alarm
• Separation
• Locked route
• Destroyed evidence
• Released creature
• Lost time
• Changed dungeon state

# PART X — PUZZLE ARCHITECTURE

## 1. PUZZLE PURPOSE

Puzzles may:

• Guard access
• Test worthiness
• Reveal lore
• Control machinery
• Represent ritual
• Create cooperation
• Reinforce theme

## 2. INFORMATION REQUIREMENTS

A puzzle must provide enough information to solve it.

The solution may be difficult, but it should not rely on guessing the DM's private association.

## 3. MULTIPLE SOLUTIONS

Allow alternatives where reasonable.

Examples:

• Solve the riddle
• Break the mechanism
• Find a bypass
• Use magic
• Negotiate with the guardian
• Accept a cost

## 4. CHARACTER KNOWLEDGE

Use checks to provide:

• Context
• Partial clues
• Cultural knowledge
• Mechanical insight
• Confirmation

Do not use one failed check to permanently block the adventure.

## 5. COOPERATIVE PUZZLES

Design tasks requiring different contributions.

Examples:

• One player translates symbols
• One operates machinery
• One holds off enemies
• One navigates changing platforms

# PART XI — EXPLORATION AND RESOURCE PRESSURE

## 1. EXPLORATION AS GAMEPLAY

Exploration requires:

• Uncertainty
• Meaningful routes
• Time pressure
• Resource decisions
• Discoverable information
• Consequences

## 2. RESOURCE CATEGORIES

• Hit points
• Spell slots
• Healing
• Light
• Food
• Water
• Ammunition
• Tools
• Time
• Safe resting locations

## 3. LIGHT

Light affects:

• Visibility
• Stealth
• Navigation
• Atmosphere
• Monster behavior

Time pressure may come from:

• Ritual countdown
• Reinforcements
• Flooding
• Hostage danger
• Disease
• Changing patrols

## 5. RANDOM ENCOUNTERS

Random encounters represent active dungeon life.

They may include:

• Patrol
• Scavenger
• Refugee
• Environmental change
• Faction messenger
• Wandering monster

## 6. RESTING

Rest should be a strategic decision.

Questions:

• Is the location secure?
• Can enemies discover the party?
• Does the dungeon change?
• Are resources consumed?
• Does the villain advance plans?

# PART XII — ENVIRONMENTAL STORYTELLING

## 1. SHOW THE PAST

Use:

• Murals
• Damage
• Tools
• Skeleton positions
• Inscriptions
• Barricades
• Abandoned meals
• Broken machinery
• Repurposed rooms

## 2. LAYERED EVIDENCE

Provide multiple clues that support the same conclusion.

Example conclusion: The defenders turned on one another.

Evidence:

• Weapons embedded in interior doors
• Bodies from both factions
• Journal fragment
• Barricades facing inward
• Poisoned water stores

## 3. CONTRADICTORY EVIDENCE

Contradictions create mystery.

Example:

• Official inscriptions praise the king
• Hidden cells contain political prisoners
• Secret chapel depicts the king as a demon

## 4. PERSONAL EVIDENCE

Small human details create emotional weight.

Examples:

• Child's drawing
• Unsent letter
• Repaired toy
• Wedding ring
• Marked calendar
• Half-finished meal

# PART XIV — ENCOUNTER PLACEMENT

## 1. ENCOUNTER PURPOSE

Every encounter should do at least one of the following:

• Drain resources
• Reveal information
• Change position
• Introduce faction
• Present choice
• Escalate danger
• Alter dungeon state

## 2. SPATIAL DISTRIBUTION

Avoid placing every encounter in isolated square rooms.

Use:

• Bridges
• Stairs
• Intersections
• Balconies
• Flooded chambers
• Moving platforms
• Multi-room fights

## 3. PATROL LOGIC

Patrols should follow routes based on:

• Importance
• Shift schedule
• Supply needs
• Communication
• Recent alarm
• Leadership orders

## 4. ENCOUNTER TELEGRAPHING

Players may detect:

• Voices
• Footprints
• Light
• Smoke
• Smell
• Shadows
• Animal reactions
• Recent damage

Information creates tactical choice.

# PART XV — REST, RETREAT, AND REINFORCEMENT

## 1. RETREAT

Retreat should remain possible unless fiction clearly prevents it.

Retreat creates:

• Strategic choice
• Fear
• Resource management
• Future consequences

## 2. DUNGEON RESPONSE TO RETREAT

After the party leaves, inhabitants may:

• Reinforce
• Relocate
• Set traps
• Flee
• Negotiate
• Complete rituals
• Destroy evidence
• Move treasure

## 3. SAFE ROOMS

A safe room should be:

• Earned
• Defensible
• Limited
• Potentially discoverable

Examples:

• Sealed shrine
• Hidden barracks
• Abandoned vault
• Friendly enclave

## 4. REINFORCEMENTS

Enemies should reinforce logically.

Questions:

• How do they communicate?
• How far away are allies?
• Who responds first?
• What routes do they use?
• What happens if alarms fail?

# PART XVI — DYNAMIC AND REACTIVE DUNGEONS

## 1. DUNGEON STATE

Track a small number of variables.

**ALERT**

```
0 — Unaware
1 — Suspicious
2 — Searching
3 — Mobilized
4 — Lockdown
```

**STRUCTURAL STABILITY**

```
Stable
Damaged
Failing
Collapsing
```

**RITUAL PROGRESS**

```
Not begun
Preparation
Active
Near completion
Complete
```

## 2. REACTION TO PLAYER ACTION

Examples:

• Loud combat raises alert
• Broken machinery floods lower level
• Freed prisoners create rebellion
• Destroyed guardian releases sealed monster
• Stolen artifact destabilizes magic

## 3. TIMED EVENTS

Examples:

• Patrol changes every hour
• Water rises
• Eclipse approaches
• Rival party advances
• Dragon returns
• Portal opens

## 4. REVISITING AREAS

Previously explored areas should change when appropriate.

Possible changes:

• New patrol
• Loot removed
• Bodies moved
• Barricade erected
• Creature feeding
• Fire spreading
• Survivors hiding

• Family history
• Patron
• Former mentor
• Cultural origin
• Oath
• Fear

## 5. SYMBOLIC LAYER

Architecture can represent theme.

**REDEMPTION** — Descent followed by ascent

**HUBRIS** — Tower built on unstable foundations

**CORRUPTION** — Orderly rooms becoming distorted

**MEMORY** — Repeated chambers with altered details

**CONTROL** — Concentric barriers and observation posts

# PART XVIII — CLIMAXES, SANCTUMS, AND FINAL CHAMBERS

## 1. THE FINAL CHAMBER

The final chamber should combine:

• Mechanical challenge
• Environmental identity
• Narrative revelation
• Player choice
• Lasting consequence

## 2. BOSS ARENA DESIGN

Include:

• Movement options
• Cover
• Hazard
• Objective beyond damage
• Changing terrain
• Weakness or interaction point

## 3. REVELATION

The climax should reveal something meaningful.

Examples:

• The villain was containing a greater threat
• The relic is sentient
• The patron caused the disaster
• The dungeon is a prison rather than a tomb

## 4. FINAL CHOICE

Possible choices:

• Destroy or preserve
• Rescue or pursue
• Claim or return
• Reveal or conceal
• Rule or liberate
• Seal or open

# PART XX — DUNGEON AUDIT SYSTEM

## 1. FUNCTION AUDIT

- [ ] The dungeon has a clear original purpose.
- [ ] Its rooms reflect that purpose.
- [ ] Current inhabitants use the space logically.
- [ ] Food, water, access, and defense are broadly explained.

## 2. NAVIGATION AUDIT

- [ ] Players have meaningful route choices.
- [ ] At least one loop or shortcut exists.
- [ ] Major zones have landmarks.
- [ ] Secret paths have functional reasons.
- [ ] Junctions provide usable information.

## 3. CHALLENGE AUDIT

- [ ] Hazards are foreshadowed.
- [ ] Traps allow counterplay.
- [ ] Encounters use terrain.
- [ ] Failure changes the situation.
- [ ] Retreat is possible where appropriate.

## 4. STORY AUDIT

- [ ] Architecture reveals history.
- [ ] Multiple clues support major conclusions.
- [ ] The dungeon connects to campaign themes.
- [ ] The central revelation changes player understanding.
- [ ] The final chamber resolves more than combat.

## 5. ECOLOGY AUDIT

- [ ] Creatures have plausible territory.
- [ ] Food or energy sources exist.
- [ ] Factions interact.
- [ ] Dungeon life changes in response to events.
- [ ] Random encounters represent active inhabitants.

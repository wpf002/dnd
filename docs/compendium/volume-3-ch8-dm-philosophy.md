# I. THE ROLE OF THE DUNGEON MASTER

The Dungeon Master is not the opponent.

The Dungeon Master is not the author.

The Dungeon Master is not the audience.

The Dungeon Master is the steward of the world.

Responsibilities include:

• Presenting situations
• Portraying NPCs
• Adjudicating rules
• Maintaining consistency
• Applying consequences
• Managing pacing
• Facilitating player agency

The DM's success is measured by the quality of the players' experience, not by the success or failure of their characters.

---

# II. THE SOCIAL CONTRACT

Every campaign rests on an agreement.

Players agree to:

• Engage with the world
• Respect other players
• Accept consequences
• Share the spotlight

The Dungeon Master agrees to:

• Be consistent
• Be impartial
• Respect player choices
• Apply rules fairly
• Communicate expectations

Trust is more valuable than perfect rules knowledge.

---

# III. FAIRNESS

Fairness is not giving players what they want.

Fairness is ensuring that:

• Rules are applied consistently.
• Information is reasonably obtainable.
• Success is possible.
• Failure follows understandable causes.
• Consequences match decisions.

The world should not bend to preserve or destroy the party.

---

# IV. IMPROVISATION

Preparation creates confidence.

Improvisation creates life.

Prepare:

• Goals
• NPCs
• Factions
• Locations
• Conflicts

Do not prepare:

• Player decisions.

When players surprise you, ask:

"What would this world logically do next?"

Not:

"How do I get them back on my plot?"

---

# V. PLAYER AGENCY

Agency requires:

• Meaningful choices
• Predictable consequences
• Multiple solutions
• Freedom to fail
• Freedom to succeed unexpectedly

Players should solve problems in ways the DM did not anticipate.

Reward creativity grounded in the fiction.

---

# VI. RULINGS VS. RULES

Rules provide consistency.

Rulings provide flexibility.

Use rules for common situations.

Use rulings for unusual situations.

A ruling should be:

• Quick
• Fair
• Consistent
• Easy to remember

If the same ruling repeats frequently, consider formalizing it as a house rule.

---

# VII. FAILURE

Failure should move the story.

Examples:

Instead of:

"The lock cannot be opened."

Use:

"The lock opens, but nearby guards hear the attempt."

Instead of:

"You fail to climb."

Use:

"You reach the ledge but drop your pack."

Failure introduces cost.

It should not routinely halt progress.

---

# VIII. CONSEQUENCES

Consequences make choices meaningful.

Types:

Immediate

Delayed

Visible

Hidden

Personal

Political

Economic

Social

Cosmic

Not every action requires dramatic fallout, but significant actions should leave lasting effects.

---

# IX. TRUST

Players trust a DM when they believe:

• The world is consistent.
• Dice matter.
• Their choices matter.
• NPCs behave logically.
• Challenges are fair.
• Success is earned.

Trust allows surprise.

Without trust, surprise feels arbitrary.

---

# X. PREPARATION

Prepare:

• Situations
• Motivations
• Maps
• Names
• Secrets
• Timelines
• Random tables

Avoid preparing exact conversations or predetermined player actions.

Expect the unexpected.

---

# XI. RUNNING THE TABLE

Monitor:

• Spotlight balance
• Session pacing
• Rules questions
• Player engagement
• Tone
• Break timing

Encourage quieter players.

Avoid allowing one player to dominate every scene.

Keep initiative moving.

If a rules question threatens momentum:

Make a temporary ruling.

Research after the session.

---

# XII. HANDLING PROBLEMS

Common issues:

Rules arguments

Spotlight imbalance

Disruptive behavior

Scheduling

Tone mismatch

Character conflict becoming player conflict

Resolve problems through conversation first.

The healthiest campaigns communicate early.

---

# XIII. CAMPAIGN LONGEVITY

Long campaigns survive through change.

Allow:

• NPC growth
• Political change
• New villains
• Character development
• Regional evolution
• Consequences

Avoid resetting the world after every adventure.

History should accumulate.

---

# XIV. THE DAVIS PRINCIPLES

1. Prepare situations, not scripts.
2. Every important NPC wants something.
3. Every location tells a story.
4. Every faction moves even without the players.
5. Consequences create immersion.
6. Player creativity is an asset, not a threat.
7. Consistency is more valuable than complexity.
8. Mystery requires discoverable truth.
9. Challenge decisions, not patience.
10. The campaign belongs to the table, not the DM.

---

# XV. DUNGEON MASTER AUDIT

- [ ] I reward creativity.
- [ ] I maintain consistency.
- [ ] I adapt instead of forcing outcomes.
- [ ] My NPCs pursue independent goals.
- [ ] My players have meaningful choices.
- [ ] Failure creates new situations.
- [ ] The world changes over time.
- [ ] Preparation supports improvisation.
- [ ] The campaign reflects player actions.
- [ ] Everyone leaves wanting to play again.

---

# THE DAVIS CODE

A great Dungeon Master does not build the perfect story.

A great Dungeon Master builds a world where unforgettable stories become inevitable.

The rules create structure.

The players create heroes.

The dice create uncertainty.

The world creates consequences.

The Dungeon Master ensures those four remain in balance.
